1. Introduction: Why Derivatives in
Microeconomics?
In economics, we rarely care about the "total" amount in isolation. We care
about change.
•

•

The Marginal Concept: Whenever you hear the word "Marginal"
(Marginal Cost, Marginal Utility, Marginal Revenue), you are talking
about a derivative.
The Slope: A derivative tells us the slope of a function at a specific point.
It answers the question: "If I change my input (Quantity) by a tiny
amount, how much does my output (Profit/Cost) change?"

2. Core Rules for the "Micro" Toolkit
You don’t need to be a mathematician to master Micro, but you must master
these three rules:

A. The Power Rule (The Bread and Butter)
If your function is f(x) = axn, then the derivative f'(x) is:
f'(x) = n*axn-1
•

In plain English: Bring the exponent down to multiply, then subtract 1
from the exponent.

B. The Constant Rule
If f(x) = c (where c is a number like 100), then f'(x) = 0.
•

Economic Logic: Fixed costs do not change when you produce one
more unit, so their "marginal" effect is zero.

C. The Sum/Difference Rule
The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives.
•

If TC = 50 + 2Q + Q2, we derive each part separately.

## CONSUMER PREFERENCES
Consumer preference theory is a theory that explains how consumers make decisions. It is based
on the idea that consumers are rational and will choose the product or service they believe will
satisfy their needs.

Consumer preference is a term that refers to consumers' choices to maximize their satisfaction.

➢ The consumer must choose a combination of goods (market basket or bundle of
goods/consumption bundles).
➢ The consumer will choose the combination that produces the most utility (satisfaction).

## MICROECONOMICS

5


---
## CONSUMER PREFERENCES
The theory of consumer preferences is based on three main assumptions:
❑ Completeness: This assumption implies that consumers can rank all combinations of goods
available to them. In other words, given any two bundles of goods A and B, consumers can
determine whether they prefer A to B, B to A, or are indifferent between the two.
❑ Transitivity: The transitivity assumption asserts that if a consumer prefers Bundle A to Bundle B,
and Bundle B to Bundle C, then the consumer will also prefer Bundle A to Bundle C. This
assumption is essential for rational consumer decision-making.
❑ Non-satiation: Otherwise known as the "more is better" assumption, non-satiation implies that
given two bundles of goods, consumers will prefer the bundle offering more of at least one good
and no less of other goods.

INDIFFERENCE CURVES and MARGINAL RATE OF SUBSTITUTION: MRS
We will often find it useful to refer to the slope of an
indifference curve at a particular point.
The slope of an indifference curve ( at each of its
points) is known as the marginal rate of substitution
## (MRS).
The MRS measures the rate at which the consumer
is willing to substitute one good for the other –
while staying inside the same indifference curve.
MRS = change in Y/change in X
(MRS = dY/dX)

## UTILITY: CONNECTION BETWEEN MARGINAL UTILITY & MARGINAL RATE OF SUBSTITUTION

The Marginal Rate of Substitution looks at the balance in changes of good X and good
Y required for the consumer to be indifferent between his/her consumption bundles
before and after trade. But what does indifference mean? It means that utility for both
bundles is exactly equal. Therefore, dU = 0
There is some (negative) change in utility resulting from giving up a little bit of good Y
which equals: MUydy
Similarly, there is some (positive) change in utility from getting a little more of good X,
which equals: MUx dx
Since we want to be indifferent before and after the trade, it must be that the sum of
these changes equals zero. Our equation would thus look like this:
dU = MUx . dx + MUy . Dy = 0

What causes an outward shift in the PPF?
Economic growth (the growing ability of the economy to produce goods and services)
results in an outward shift of the Production Possibilities Frontier because production
possibilities are expanded.
There are two sources of economic growth:
•An increase in the quantity or quality of

factors of production (the resources to
produce goods and services).
•Progress in technology (the technical

means for the production of goods and services).

Microeconomics

---
What causes an inward shift in the PPF?
A PPF will shift inwards when an economy has suffered a loss or exhaustion of some of its
scarce resources. This reduces an economy's productive potential.

What causes an asymmetric shift in the PPF?
An economy can grow because of an increase in productivity in one sector of the economy
- this is called asymmetric growth.

Demand Curve
• The buyers, as a group, determine the demand for
the product or service.

Demand function:

Qdx = f (Px, Py, … Pz, I, T, E, Nb)
Ceteris Paribus

• The behaviour of buyers is described by the law
of demand.

Px: price of good x

The law of demand says (ceteris paribus = other things
being equal, remaining the same): as the price of a good
rises, the quantity demanded declines and vice versa.
Qdx = f(Px)

Py, Pz: price of related products (substitutes, complements)
I: Income ( for normal goods, for inferior goods)
T: Tastes and Preferences for that good (impact of marketing efforts)
E: Expectation of future price
Nb: Number of buyers

Consumer Surplus
For the customers, this measure of trade gain is
called consumer surplus.
❖ For a single consumer, the individual consumer
surplus is the difference between the maximum
price (reservation price) that she/he is willing to
pay for a given amount of a good or service and
the price he or she actually pays.
❖ A consumer’s willingness to pay for a good or
service is the maximum price at which they would
buy that good or service.

❖ Total consumer surplus is the sum of the
individual consumer surpluses of all buyers of a
good or service in a market.
❖ The consumer surplus is measured by the area
below the market demand curve and above the
equilibrium price.

Producer Surplus
For the producers/sellers, the measure of trade gain obtained
from selling a good or
service at the equilibrium price is called producer surplus.
❖

Individual producer surplus is the net gain to an individual
seller from selling a good.

❖

It is equal to the difference between the price received and
the seller’s cost (the minimum price at which the seller is
willing to sell).

❖

Cost: the value of everything a seller must give up to produce
a good.

❖

The producer surplus for sellers is the amount that sellers
benefit from by selling at a market price that is higher than the
minimum price they would be willing to sell for.

❖

Total producer surplus is the sum of the individual producer
surpluses of all the sellers of a good in a market.

❖

The producer surplus is measured by the area above the
market supply curve and below the equilibrium price.

6.4 Monopolistic competition versus perfect competition
• The term monopolistic competition was coined by the economist
Edward Chamberlain (Harvard University) in 1930.
• According to Gregory Mankiw: “monopolistic competition is a market
structure in which many companies sell similar, but not identical,
products”.
• Samuelson and Nordhaus: ¨Many sellers offer goods that are close
substitutes, but not perfect substitutes¨.

6.4 Monopolistic competition versus perfect competition
Characteristics
 Differentiated product: Firms compete by selling
differentiated products that are easily substituted by the
other varieties.
 Many firms: Each firm is a monopolist of its variety and
determines the level of production that maximizes its profits
(MgR(q*)=MgC(q*)). The firm has monopoly power since it
can influence the price of its own variety.
 No barriers: There are no entry or exit barriers for firms.

6.4 Monopolistic competition versus perfect competition
A monopolistic competition market is similar to a perfect competition
market in two key aspects:
 There are many firms
 The entry of new firms is not limited.
But it is different in:
 The product is differentiated.

Each firm looks for a brand or version of the product that differs by its
quality, its appearance or its reputation, and each firm is the only
producer of its own brand / variety.
The degree of monopoly power that the firms has depends on its
success in differentiating its product from the other companies.

competition.
• A market is efficient if social welfare (SW) is maximized.
• The SW reaches its maximum when the value of the last unit consumed
(which is given by the demand curve) equals the MgC of producing it.
• The difference between the maximum social welfare and the effective
social welfare is known as the irrecoverable loss of efficiency.
• A monopoly can generate irrecoverable loss of efficiency as long as it
exchanges less than the efficient quantity.

## 3.1 CONSUMER DEMAND
## COMPLEMENTARY, SUBSTITUTE OR INDEPENDENT GOODS

What happens to the consumption of a good when the price
of another good changes?
When the price of a good decreases, the consumer´s purchasing
power increases, then the quantity demanded of other goods may
increase. We can talk about 3 different types of relationship
between goods:
❑ Two goods are complementary if the variation in the price of
one of them causes a variation in the opposite direction of the
quantity demanded of the other.

❑ Two goods are substitutes if the variation in the price of one of
them causes a variation in the same direction of the quantity
demanded of the other.
❑ Two goods are independent if the variation in the price of one
of them does not affect the quantity demanded of the other.
